# Simplified technical English for newsroom and technical prose

When to read this: Use these rules when a procedure, correction, explainer, or technical report must be easy to act on.

## Purpose

ASD-STE100 is a controlled language for technical documentation.
This reference adapts its clarity methods for journalism and software work.
It does not claim that ordinary prose complies with the full standard.
Use the official standard when formal compliance is required.

## Core rules

1. Put one instruction or main idea in each sentence.
2. Keep sentences short enough to understand on the first read.
3. Use the active voice when the actor matters.
4. Name the actor before the action.
5. Use the same term for the same thing.
6. Give one approved meaning to each recurring term.
7. Prefer common words over formal substitutes.
8. Put conditions before the action when the condition controls the action.
9. Put warnings before the step that creates the risk.
10. Remove words that do not change the instruction or claim.

## Sentence length

For formal STE, use no more than 20 words in a procedural sentence.
Use no more than 25 words in a descriptive sentence.
For prose that does not claim STE compliance, treat these lengths as warning points rather than automatic limits.
Split a sentence when it contains two actions, two conditions, or two conclusions.
Keep a longer sentence only when splitting it would hide a necessary relationship.

Before:

> After the editor approves the story and the producer confirms the images, update the page and notify the audience team.

After:

> Get the editor's approval. Confirm the images with the producer. Then update the page and notify the audience team.

## Optional house style (not part of STE)

Sentence-case headings and capitalization after a colon are house-style choices.
They are not ASD-STE100 rules.
Follow the publication's style guide, such as AP or Chicago, when it differs.

## One instruction per sentence

Do not join independent commands with `and`.
Use a numbered list when order matters.
Use bullets when order does not matter.

Before:

> Export the data, remove duplicate rows, and check the total against the source.

After:

1. Export the data.
2. Remove duplicate rows.
3. Check the total against the source.

## Active voice and named actors

Active voice shows who must act and who made a claim.
Passive voice is acceptable when the actor is unknown, irrelevant, or protected.
Do not use passive voice to hide responsibility.

Weak:

> It was decided that the correction would be published.

Clear:

> The managing editor approved the correction.

## Controlled terms

Choose one term for each important object, role, status, and action.
Do not alternate between `article`, `story`, and `piece` if they mean the same record.
Define an unavoidable technical term at first use.
Keep official names, quoted language, legal terms, and source terminology exact.

Example glossary:

| Use | Avoid as a synonym |
| --- | --- |
| story | article, piece, item |
| publish | push live, ship, release |
| source record | original item, base entry |
| verify | validate, confirm, check off |

## Approved-word discipline

Prefer a small set of plain verbs: `use`, `make`, `start`, `stop`, `check`, `send`, and `remove`.
Do not replace a precise domain term with a simpler but incorrect word.
Do not rewrite direct quotations to fit the controlled vocabulary.
In news copy, preserve meaning, attribution, uncertainty, and the speaker's register.

## Conditions, warnings, and results

Put a condition first when it determines whether the reader must act.

> If the totals do not match, stop the import.

Put the action first when the condition is only supporting detail.

> Save the receipt in the case file after the upload finishes.

Use a colon when the second part explains or lists the first part.

> Result: The import stopped before it changed any records.

## Register calibration

Tighten the rules for procedures, safety notes, corrections, data methods, and handoffs.
These texts must support one correct interpretation and one clear next action.

Loosen the rules for features, profiles, criticism, and narrative scenes.
Vary rhythm and sentence length when that variation carries voice, pace, or emphasis.
Keep factual claims, attribution, terminology, and chronology precise.

Do not force controlled language into quotations or literary passages.
Do not use style variation as permission for vague claims.

## Final check

- Does each sentence have one main job?
- Is the actor clear where responsibility matters?
- Does each recurring term keep one meaning?
- Can the reader find every condition before acting?
- Are warnings placed before the risky step?
- Does the text follow the publication's heading and colon style?
- Did the edit preserve the source's meaning and uncertainty?
