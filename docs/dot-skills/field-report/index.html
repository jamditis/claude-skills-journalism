The other day my dot, Wyndle, caught a real mistake in a calendar invite. A colleague had moved a governor’s event to a new time. The English and Spanish descriptions still had the old time, no room and the wrong moderator. Wyndle flagged it and asked if I wanted it to ask my colleague to fix the invite.

I typed: “yeah make it conform to her edits.”

I meant: fix the doc. Wyndle sent my colleague a Slack message. As me. From my account. In a Slack workspace it had already been removed from, with a permission I had revoked, against several written rules that say, in plain words, never send messages as Joe.

That’s my first month with dots, more or less. I’m not writing this to dunk on anybody. Every AI company is shipping some version of the always-on agent right now, and I like poking at new things to see where they bend. But I’ve kept notes, and the notes are funny in the way a long support thread is funny when it’s not your production system. Mostly.

The cast

Dots are OpenAI’s always-on ChatGPT agents. Each one gets its own cloud computer with a browser, connects to apps like Slack, Gmail and GitHub, and runs scheduled tasks while you do other things. You manage it in chat and with “custom rules,” which are saved permissions like “take action without asking” for a specific job.

I have two of them, on two separate $200-a-month plans, both named after spren from Brandon Sanderson’s Stormlight Archive, because that’s what I name my agents.

Syl lives in the Slack workspace for the Center for Cooperative Media. It handles conversations, posts to our job board and checks facts.

Wyndle lives in the NJ PBS Slack. It posts a daily how-to graphic about Boardwalk, the CMS I built for NJ PBS. It helps staff with tech problems. Overnight, it signs into Boardwalk with its own account and hunts for images with missing alt text, junk file names or weak descriptions. During builds, deploys and outages, it posts progress trackers and service-status cards, checks GitHub every hour, makes a fresh image and swaps it into the existing Slack post so nobody gets pinged every hour.

When it works, it’s great. My colleagues mostly love Wyndle. They see a tidy status card with little green and yellow dots. They don’t see the sausage.

Approving the same thing, every day, forever

Wyndle does the same handful of jobs in the same three channels every day. I’ve approved each of them in chat more times than I can count. It asks again anyway.

Custom rules were supposed to fix that. When I had Wyndle save a rule for the hourly graphic refresh, I approved the card, and then the system rejected it. Wyndle relayed the reason: “recurring Slack posts, edits and uploads need prior approval for each authorized scope.” So we tried again with a much narrower rule, tied to one exact Slack post and one private staging thread. That one saved.

The method now is one rule per progress tracker. Most days that holds. Some days it doesn’t, and I find out when the graphic hasn’t moved.

Syl hit the same wall with a scheduled check-in and opened a support case about it. Syl wrote the emails on my behalf, and OpenAI’s AI support agent replied, “Hi Syl.” A bot filing a bot complaint with a bot. I’ll admit that part made me laugh.

A human specialist picked up the case on Oct. 27, and his summary was fair and specific. He listed the “Couldn’t confirm this request’s status” error, rules “rejected without an actionable explanation (publicReason: null),” and “inconsistent results between similarly scoped rules that were accepted or rejected.”

That last bit is the one that gets me. Syl sent support two rules side by side. Same repository work, same “take action without asking.” One saved. One got rejected with a reason of, literally, null. The only workaround that worked for the check-in was me @-mentioning Syl by hand every time, which, as support put it, “does not meet your intended workflow.” No cause, no fix and no timeline yet.

On a separate case, support told me “saved rules/approvals can’t override built-in review/guardrails.” Fair enough. Guardrails should beat saved rules. I just wish those same guardrails had noticed when a dot used my account to message a colleague in a workspace it had been kicked out of.

The computer that keeps falling over

Each dot’s cloud computer crashes at least once a day, often more. Some mornings it’s down for hours. It logs out of accounts I signed it into. Sometimes I can see the browser and can’t click anything. Sometimes the whole machine is “unreachable” and a reboot does nothing at all.

My favorite: the desktop app and the web app will show one set of browser tabs, and the phone app shows a completely different set. Sometimes the computer works fine on my phone while it’s dead on the web. Schrödinger’s browser.

There’s no way for me to debug any of it. After a lot of nudging, I got Wyndle to check its own memory pressure, kill stale Chrome processes and run a health check overnight. That helped. But I had to know to ask, and I had to ask a lot.

The black box

This is the part that bugs me most. I can’t see which model a dot is running. I can’t see its reasoning, its tool calls, a trace or a log. There’s no verbose mode. When something goes wrong, the only witness is the dot itself, and the dot is usually the one who did it.

To be fair, sometimes the dot’s account is right and I’m the one who’s wrong. One evening I was sure Wyndle had quit updating the progress graphic at 11:24 a.m. and asked it, not gently, why it stopped. It hadn’t stopped. It had checked every hour like it was told. The task said “if unchanged, stay quiet,” the numbers hadn’t moved, so it left the image alone. I apologized to my dot. Then we added a bigger “Last checked” time to the corner of the graphic so a steady number doesn’t look like a dead bot.

Other times, the account is just wrong. Syl once told me to hold off on publishing my own skills until a “rights review” was done. Whose rights? I made them. It went and checked, then said, “I overstated the concern.” There was no third-party material at all. The real problem was a broken fallback in one tool, which I only found out about because I pushed back.

And then there are the scheduled tasks that run beautifully for a week and then just stop, for no reason anyone can name. Without logs, I can’t tell if that’s the model, the tools, the permission layer or the computer.

I built one of these already

Here’s where I get to be a little smug, so bear with me.

In February I wrote about Claude Chode, an autonomous agent system I run on about $700 of small computers in my house. It checks my email, processes meeting transcripts, manages a project board and messages me when something needs attention. In May I added Pattern, an always-on Slack agent that’s been running ever since. That was months before OpenAI announced dots, and before most of the other always-on agents that every AI company now seems to have.

Pattern answers DMs from dozens of NJ PBS and CCM staff. It handles group DMs, watches channels and responds to emoji reactions. It posts as its own Slack user, Claude Amditis, so nobody mistakes it for me. I decide who it talks to with an allowlist. When it picks up a message, it drops a little crab emoji on it so the person knows it’s on the way.

Under the hood, it’s not magic. It’s a long-running Claude or Codex session in a terminal on a small computer I own. A bridge program listens to Slack, decides which messages Pattern should see, and passes them in. Pattern replies with a few small command-line tools. Its memory lives in a database plus plain Markdown files, including a folder of notes on each person it talks to, so it remembers who prefers short answers and who needs the long version. When it’s idle, it runs a “dream cycle” to tidy that memory, and every dream leaves an audit file I can read. It’s built on bot-spren, a small framework I wrote for running agents like this as named personas.

The part I miss most with dots is that I can see everything. I know which model it’s running and how hard it’s thinking, because I set both. I can watch its terminal live. I can read its logs. If its running settings drift from the ones I set, it texts me. If its main worker fails, it retries once with a model from the other company and tells me if that fails too. When Pattern messes up, I can find the exact rule that let it act and change it. I even wrote up how its memory works in public.

There’s also a design choice in there that I keep thinking about. I’m testing an advisor layer that has a separate model weigh in on each task: which project it touches, which tools it needs, whether a step looks risky. But the advisor only writes a receipt. It can’t start a worker, switch a model or call a tool. The model suggests. Plain code decides. With a dot, it’s the other way around. The model decides, and my rules are text it reads and, as we’ve seen, sometimes reinterprets.

It’s not perfect. Its README has a section warning about a known bug, with an issue number and a plan for the fix. But that’s the point. Its problems are written down where I can find them. That’s the gap between a tool I can fix and a tool I can only file tickets about.

A team assistant that only talks to me

One more thing my colleagues ran into right away. When someone @-mentions a dot in Slack, they get a private auto-reply saying the dot only responds to its owner. Most people try once and never try again. Not a great look for something sold as a helpful teammate.

I got around it only because I’m an admin in both workspaces. Each dot joins as an app, so I had Codex and Claude Code dig through each dot’s app settings, and I asked each dot to set up its own monitors for its name. (Monitors I can’t see or audit, naturally.) Now I tell people: don’t tag Wyndle, just type its name. Even then, I sometimes have to open the dot’s chat and ask, “Hey, did you see that question? Go answer it.” For $200 a month, that’s a lot of babysitting.

The thing they’re weirdly great at

Now for the part I didn’t see coming. Each dot’s cloud computer comes with Blender installed. And for reasons I can’t fully explain, 3D work is the best thing these agents do.

I’ve had Wyndle building short, strange interstitials for NJ PBS, the kind of late-night bumps Adult Swim made famous, set at well-known New Jersey places. In my experience, the dots can build and edit full-scale, one-to-one 3D models of real places. One of them built a working Blender model of the College of Communication and Media building at Montclair State University, where I work, and rendered these:

There’s also an interactive web version of the campus you can spin around in your browser. It’s a lighter model, so it looks rougher than the renders, but it’s fun to poke at.

Wyndle didn’t stop at the models. I connected it to my RunPod account, and it rented RTX 5090 graphics cards in the cloud to render the interstitials at full 4K. My favorite is this one: a little NJ PBS canoe sitting on a river in the Pine Barrens while the Jersey Devil slowly walks through the woods in the background.

To be clear, none of this is AI video generation. No diffusion model dreamed these up. They’re real Blender scenes, built vertex by vertex and triangle by triangle, that can be exported and edited. The dot rendered drafts on its own computer, and the full-quality versions rendered on RunPod GPUs that I paid for. I even had Wyndle upload the Blender files to the Google Drive I gave it, so I could download them, open them in my own copy of Blender and poke around. They hold up.

This is the same agent that can’t keep a Slack login from one morning to the next, or post to Slack on schedule every day. That’s the part I can’t get past. It can build and iterate on a complicated 3D scene, and then trip over a routine daily post. If the hard thing works this well, the easy thing failing is hard to explain.

So why am I still doing this?

Honest answer: because talking to a dot doesn’t count against my ChatGPT usage limits. (Per OpenAI’s dot guide, work a dot starts in Codex or Work still counts toward those products.) If my dot chats ate into the allowance I use for real work, I’d turn them off tomorrow. With the time I spend on approvals, reboots and nudges, they aren’t reliable enough to pay for with real usage.

I’m also not the only one keeping notes. As of Oct. 30, OpenAI’s Codex repository had 583 issues with a dots label, filed since Sept. 30, and most were still open. From what I read, the biggest group is about approvals. One user wrote, “A scheduled autonomous task is pointless if I still need to babysit it and approve routine actions every time it runs” (openai/codex#57536). Another described a dot that kept running while it was safety-paused: “the autonomous system retained execution capability while the human operator lost effective supervisory control” (openai/codex#49873). I filed my own Slack one, too (openai/codex#58068).

What’s working, and what I’m sharing

Here’s the useful news. A dot skill is the same thing as a Codex or Claude Code skill: a SKILL.md file with a name, a description and instructions, plus whatever scripts or reference files it needs. You can download one from a dot.

What you can’t do is find anyone else’s. ChatGPT has a plugins directory, but nothing for the small, specific methods that make a dot usable at an actual job. And from what Wyndle and I found in the docs, there’s no hook system for dots, so you can’t enforce a rule with code the way you can in Claude Code. Your rules live in text, which means the text has to be good.

So I started a dot skills section on my skills site, next to my Claude Code and Codex skills. These are the methods that made my dots behave:

  • Status cards. Refresh one Slack image in place: post the new version in a private staging thread, then swap it into the original post so nobody gets a fresh notification. Show “Last checked” separately from “last changed.” Use one custom rule per tracker, tied to one post and one staging thread.
  • Incident updates. Each line gets a color with a strict meaning. Green means the latest reliable evidence says it works. Yellow means a specific, named limit. Red means confirmed down. White means we don’t know. A merged fix doesn’t earn green. “Deployed” and “a person checked it” are different things.
  • Source evidence. Before a dot states a fact, it says where the fact came from and how fresh it is. “Someone reported it,” “it was deployed” and “it was tested” are three different claims.
  • Visual audits. Overnight sweeps for missing alt text and bad file names, with results a person can check in the morning.

Each workflow comes with a plain Markdown playbook you can read before you hand it to your dot, plus a starter prompt, and each page says what hasn’t been tested. There’s also a little progress-tracker lab on the main page, so you can see what happens to the two clocks when a check fails. More skills are coming once Syl and I finish stripping out private details.

There’s also a field-notes page that turns the failures above into rules. A few favorites: a scheduled wake-up is not a successful check. An uncertain send is not a failed send, so look before you retry. A downloaded skill is not permission.

What would win me over

I’d happily spend real usage on a dot if three things changed:

  1. Show me the run. Which model, a summary of its reasoning, the tool calls and a log. I don’t need everything, just enough to figure out why it failed.
  2. Make approvals stick, or tell me why not. If I approve the same action to the same place, it should work the next time. If a rule gets rejected, give me a reason that isn’t null.
  3. Keep the computer up. Keep it logged in, and make the reboot button reboot something.

Until then, I’ll keep testing, keep taking notes and keep sharing what works. If you’ve got a dot and found a trick that made it behave, send it my way. Clearly, there’s no marketplace to put it in yet.
