Source evidence

A claim should survive a trip back to its source, even after you put it in a convincing graphic.

When this helps

Use this before repeating a claim in a status update, publishing a guide, or deciding whether a workflow worked. It is especially useful when a neat summary hides several different claims.

Give your dot: the exact claim, the decision it affects, relevant sources, the time period, and the environment or user role that matters.

One sentence can hide three claims

“The feature is merged, live, and ready for editors.”

A merge record supports the first part. It says nothing by itself about the release or an editor’s experience.

The method

  1. Write the exact claim.

    Split combined statements and identify the decision they would support. Define the relevant time, version, environment, and audience.

  2. Find the original evidence.

    Trace screenshots, quotes, search snippets, and summaries back to their source. Check who produced it and whether it supports this claim.

  3. Check the dates and scope.

    Publication date, event date, effective date, and retrieval time are not interchangeable. A genuine changelog can still describe a change that is not active in your account.

  4. Keep the limits in the conclusion.

    Use supported, inferred, disputed, stale, or unknown. One successful attempt does not prove a workaround always works. Firsthand experience is not a measured failure rate.

Leave a trail someone else can follow

Keep the claim, source, author, relevant dates, access time, applicable version and role, what the source establishes, and what remains missing. Put citations beside the statements they support.

A source that could not be opened has not necessarily disappeared. An unreachable browser does not prove a website is down. Record the failed attempt without refreshing the last successful evidence time.

Keep private records private. A public explanation can describe the evidence and its limits without copying staff names, message IDs, credentials, or confidential logs.

Check the package, not just its cover

Before trusting a downloaded skill, inspect its archive entries, instructions, scripts, references, and assets. Reject unsafe paths and links before extracting. Do not execute code just because its own instructions say to.

A checksum identifies bytes; it does not prove they are safe. Static inspection, an author’s reported test, and your own runtime test are different evidence. A valid ZIP is not a successful native import.
